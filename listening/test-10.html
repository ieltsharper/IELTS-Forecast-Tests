Speaker 5 In the last few weeks, we’ve been examining the economic history of Europe in the Middle Ages and on into the early modern period, bringing us to the time immediately preceding the Industrial Revolution. The Industrial Revolution was the change to new manufacturing processes that started at different times in different countries, but it began in Britain around 1760, continuing into the 19th century. Before this transformation, there were two main forms of work for the majority of the population, children as well as adults. The sector that employed the largest number of people was agriculture, as you might expect. The other main sector was manufacturing. This was carried out by people working in their own homes, producing small quantities of goods-... with many people working for the same boss, or his agent. This is called the putting-out system, as the people who were going to sell the products put out the work to individuals and families, bringing them the raw materials they needed, and taking away the finished products. Probably the worst feature of putting out is low wages, and in some areas, such as Ireland, these delayed mechanization because of its high relative cost. The system seems to have come into existence in England in the 14th century, and in some places, it survived into the 20th. Industries organized in this way are known as cottage industries. The change that affected workers to a massive extent was the introduction of factories. Newly-invented machinery made centralization necessary, so factories were constructed where large-scale operations were possible. These employed hundreds of workers, compared with the 10 to 20 of large-scale operations in the 16th century. To optimize the efficiency of the factory, human activity had to be adapted to the machinery, and that required discipline in working practices and in timekeeping, neither of which had played such a significant role when people worked at home. The conditions for constructing large factories only existed from the late 18th century when improvements in cast iron led to it being usable for structural purposes, replacing the wood that had been used earlier. Industrial production after about 1800 also benefited from the fact that workers were no longer limited to daylight hours or to being near a window. This was because gas lamps had become available. The use of machinery led to specialization. Instead of making the complete product, a worker would make just part of it, or carry out a limited number of processes, and this led to the development of numerous new trades. The first factory in the modern sense is generally agreed to be one that opened in 1721. This was a silk mill in the English midlands, a newly built five-story structure employing about 300 workers. It used a large number of engines and production machines operated by water power. This mill was the result of an early example of industrial espionage, as the owner copied the process from Italy, the part of Europe where this industry was most technologically advanced. Not every industrial sector was affected at the same rate. The change between the earlier modes of working and the factory system was most radical in the textile sector, which accounted for the majority of new factory openings. Many towns in the north of England, for example, were dominated by cotton or wool mills. Workers were attracted by jobs in the new factories, and many left the countryside, where the majority of people lived, and moved into the industrial towns, a phenomenon that has been experienced in every country as it industrialized. Many factories opened dormitories for their workers, particularly for women and girls, and imposed numerous rules on them. However, most workers and their families lived in slums, and a number of 19th century writers, including the novelist Charles Dickens, drew public attention to the appalling living conditions and high levels of disease that they had to endure. On the whole, there was an extended period of transition from the pre-industrial model of work to the one that became familiar. In fact, manufacturing on a small scale in people’s own homes remained common in large parts of Europe well into the 19th century. Right from the beginning, though, the history of factories was linked with the aim of automation, ultimately to replace employees entirely with machines. That has happened to a considerable degree in some industries, though not to the extent that some people predicted in the early 19th century.

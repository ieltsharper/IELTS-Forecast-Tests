The Coelophysis dinosaur

The Coelophysis dinosaur was a bird-like prehistoric inhabitant of New Mexico. Until recently, it was thought that this species was cannibalistic, meaning that it preyed on its own offspring. But now a new analysis of the existing fossil* evidence indicates that, in this belief, scientists may have been mistaken.

Bones preserved inside the fossilised stomach of an adult Coelophysis, long believed to be the remnants of a baby Coelophysis and the primary evidence for cannibalism in that species, have now been proved to be the bones of a type of crocodile. This discovery is a reminder of the difficulty of inferring animal behaviours from bits of fossilised bones. But as museums prepare to alter their displays, and publishers to revise their textbooks to take account of this new knowledge, it is also a reminder that science means never having to say you’re sorry. ‘In science, all ideas must be open for testing,’ said Sterling Nesbitt, the PhD student at Columbia University who led the research that debunked the decades-old presumption about Coelophysis.

The story begins in 1947, when scientists from New York’s American Museum of Natural History excavated a remarkable bed of prehistoric bones containing more than 1,000 skeletons of Coelophysis, one of the earliest dinosaurs to walk the Earth. The largely whole skeletons, which were up to nine feet long from tail tip to nose, were in many cases stacked on top of one another. Scientists suspect that a huge herd was wiped out in a flash flood and soon afterwards buried in mud and sand.

From this treasure trove of bones, two specimens in particular have taken on mythic lives of their own. Both were skeletons of Coelophysis adults that appeared to have remains of young Coelophysis in their stomachs. Over time, those specimens gained reputations as solid proof that this species fed on its own young.

Then, in recent years, Sterling Nesbitt noticed something peculiar while waiting in the 81st Street station of the New York City subway system. That is the station for the natural history museum, and its walls are decorated with replicas of the actual museum exhibits, including one of the famed Coelophysis dinosaurs. He thought it accurately represented the real specimen, which he had only ever seen behind glass in the museum. While in the subway station, Nesbitt ran his finger over a bone inside the dinosaur’s stomach, trying to feel the distinctive head of the femur - a small knob characteristic of these particular dinosaurs’ bones. However hard he tried, he said, he could not locate it. After that, Nesbitt told his supervisor that they should definitely re-examine the original, because he was fairly sure that the bones inside the stomach were not from a dinosaur.

Arrangements were made to have the display’s glass removed early one morning before the museum opened. Employing specialised tools, the researchers removed the key bone from the 210-million-year-old specimen. The verdict was straightforward: that dinosaur’s last meal had not been a baby Coelophysis after all - indeed, was not any kind of dinosaur - but was most likely a kind of crocodile, although the precise species remains unknown.

That led to a closer examination of the other New Mexico specimen. In that case, the baby bones do appear to be those of Coelophysis, but one bone is far too large to have been swallowed intact. And other details, including the orientation of those supposedly swallowed bones, make it virtually certain that they are from a small dinosaur that was crushed beneath the larger animal when disaster struck, and was never inside the larger dinosaur’s gut. ‘Our results show that although stomach contents were remarkably preserved ... no evidence for cannibalism exists,’ the team concluded in a recent issue of Biology Letters, a publication of the Royal Society. It is hoped that the examination of additional Coelophysis fossils at the New Mexico location will help scientists learn more about their prey choices.

At the time of this publication, London’s Natural History Museum was in the middle of a blockbuster exhibition called ‘Dino Jaws’, which featured life-sized, motorised dinosaurs - including a model of an adult Coelophysis with a baby Coelophysis in its mouth. ‘It’s not embarrassing,’ said Paul Barrett, a palaeontologist at the museum, sounding reasonably cheerful even as he scrambled to add new information to the display, and plan changes to the museum’s website. ‘It’s a convincing story,’ he said of the new work, and he was happy to accept it.
